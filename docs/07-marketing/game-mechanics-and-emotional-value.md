# Game mechanics and emotional value

## 1. Design objective

Emotions are already part of a purchase dilemma: desire, uncertainty, anticipation, self-image, social proof, relief, pride, regret, and curiosity. Before I Buy can turn those emotions into meaningful participation and paid value, but should not intensify insecurity merely to increase revenue.

The useful question is not “How do we exploit vanity?” It is:

> How can status, expression, satisfaction, and influence help users tell a better story about who they are and how well they understand one another?

## 2. Emotional value map

| Emotion/motive | Healthy user need | Product mechanic | Monetizable layer | Red line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vanity | Express taste and competence | Profile frames, category identity, elegant recaps | Themes, recap styles, premium profile customization | Selling accuracy, visibility, or superiority |
| Satisfaction | Feel closure and progress | Reveal, completed-loop collection, “would choose again” memory | Advanced recaps, archive, export, celebration packs | Rewarding purchase volume or spending |
| Influence | Know that one's perspective helped | Helpful-reason reactions, category calibration, creator circles | Creator Pro, paid communities, appreciation gifts | Paying to alter votes, reach, or outcomes |
| Belonging | Share rituals with close people | Circles, inside reactions, group recaps, cooperative challenges | Circle Pass, community memberships | Excluding free members from outcomes |
| Curiosity | Discover what happened | Delayed Reveal, prediction resolution | Premium history around past patterns | Paywalling a Reveal or using deceptive teasers |
| Reciprocity | Thank someone for attention | Thank-you notes, giftable cosmetics, appreciation items | One-time gifts | Guilt prompts or public gift-value rankings |
| Mastery | Improve judgment over time | Calibration, resolved predictions, category specialties | Advanced insights and private trend reports | Global monetary leaderboard or false precision |
| Autonomy | Feel deliberate rather than controlled | Pause, private self-prediction, explicit reflection | Convenience and organization tools | Shame, forced delays, or coercive reminders |

## 3. Vanity without toxicity

Vanity can be a legitimate form of self-expression. Users may enjoy being seen as stylish, thoughtful, technically informed, practical, adventurous, or unusually good at understanding a friend.

### Vanity mechanics

- **Category identity cards:** “Your friends know your tech instincts” or “You are most calibrated on travel choices,” shown only after a meaningful sample.
- **Profile and circle cosmetics:** tasteful frames, typography, color systems, Reveal themes, and reaction sets.
- **Decision-year artifacts:** a polished annual card showing completed reflections, favorite categories, and memorable reversals.
- **Collections:** privately curate “best purchases,” “glad I waited,” “surprising skips,” and “friends called it.”
- **Shareable proof with consent:** user chooses which aggregate card to share; exact prices, reasons, and private friend data remain hidden.

### Vanity monetization

- premium visual themes;
- annual recap design packs;
- custom circle identity;
- physical print or high-resolution export later;
- creator-designed cosmetic collaborations with revenue share.

### Vanity guardrails

- never rank users by money spent or hypothetical money saved;
- never sell a “better predictor” badge;
- paid styling must be visually expressive, not falsely authoritative;
- public sharing is optional and previewed before export;
- no body, wealth, or lifestyle-shame language.

## 4. Satisfaction and closure

The app's deepest emotional payoff is not the vote. It is the moment the owner can say, “Now I know how this choice felt in real life.”

### Satisfaction mechanics

- **Closed-loop shelf:** completed decisions displayed as a satisfying collection.
- **Expectation versus reality:** compare owner prediction, friend aggregate, and lived outcome privately.
- **Reflection milestones:** recognize five, ten, or twenty completed loops, not consecutive-day activity.
- **Reversal stories:** celebrate when waiting changed the decision or when a purchase was unexpectedly excellent.
- **Seasonal review:** “What brought lasting satisfaction this season?”

### Satisfaction monetization

- deeper historical comparison;
- beautifully designed seasonal and annual reports;
- export and durable archive;
- premium narrative summaries after sufficient data;
- giftable printed or digital retrospective.

### Satisfaction guardrails

- a Buy and a Skip can both be satisfying;
- returned, unavailable, alternative, and gift outcomes remain first-class;
- milestones count completed reflection, not purchase volume;
- do not invent claims about money saved.

## 5. Influence without purchasing pressure

Influence is a powerful social reward: people enjoy knowing their judgment mattered. The app should reward useful perspective rather than obedience.

### Influence mechanics

- **Helpful perspective:** after reflection, the owner can privately or publicly-in-circle mark a reason as helpful.
- **Calibration by category:** show where predictions align with outcomes, with minimum sample thresholds.
- **“Made me think” outcome:** separates influence from whether the owner followed the vote.
- **Creator decision rooms:** a creator hosts themed dilemmas and teaches a decision process.
- **Advice portfolios:** creators or trusted friends can curate their most useful resolved discussions.

### Influence monetization

- Creator Pro tools;
- paid Creator Circles;
- appreciation gifts after Reveal;
- premium presentation and analytics for community hosts;
- creator-designed templates and cosmetics.

### Influence guardrails

- do not tell a voter “They ignored you”;
- do not reward convincing someone to buy;
- paid users cannot boost their vote weight;
- commercial partners cannot suppress negative outcomes;
- helpfulness is contextual and owner-controlled, not a universal reputation score.

## 6. Belonging and group identity

The app can become a lightweight ritual inside existing friend groups: everyone knows who is careful with technology, who spots fashion regret, and who always predicts that a friend will eventually buy the thing anyway.

### Belonging mechanics

- named circles with a visual identity;
- private group recap;
- inside-joke reaction packs;
- cooperative completion goals such as “close ten loops together”;
- rotating category host;
- group “season” with an end date and recap;
- celebration of diverse roles rather than one winner.

### Belonging monetization

- Circle Pass paid by one sponsor;
- group cosmetic packs;
- seasonal challenge packs;
- creator or artist collaborations;
- optional physical group recap later.

### Belonging guardrails

- no streak loss for a group member who stops participating;
- no pressure to create fake dilemmas to complete a season;
- non-paying members retain voting and Reveal access;
- circle administrators cannot access owner-private fields;
- leave, block, and mute remain free.

## 7. Curiosity and delayed Reveal

Curiosity is already the core return mechanic. It can support conversion, but the outcome belongs to participants.

### Curiosity mechanics

- reminder that a prediction will resolve later;
- one-dilemma guest Reveal opt-in;
- private archive of past predictions;
- “surprise outcome” collections after resolution;
- periodic recap of unresolved versus resolved predictions.

### Curiosity monetization

- premium archive search and pattern analysis;
- long-range recap;
- cosmetic Reveal presentation;
- cross-circle comparison after minimum samples.

### Curiosity guardrails

- never charge to open a Reveal;
- never obscure whether a subscription is required;
- no false countdown or “last chance” when nothing expires;
- do not send variable-reward notifications unrelated to an actual user action;
- guest Reveal consent remains specific to one dilemma.

## 8. Reciprocity and appreciation

Advice requires attention. A small thank-you can strengthen the social loop when it is optional and proportionate.

### Reciprocity mechanics

- free thank-you note or reaction after Reveal;
- gift a cosmetic pack to a friend or circle;
- owner-selected “this helped me think” recognition;
- paid appreciation item for eligible creators or hosts.

### Reciprocity guardrails

- free appreciation must be prominent and sufficient;
- do not prompt a gift after disappointment, distress, or regret;
- do not display who paid more;
- cap frequency and value during early experiments;
- show price and refund conditions before confirmation.

## 9. Mastery and reputation

Mastery can create long-term value if it teaches calibration rather than certainty.

### Mastery mechanics

- resolved confident-prediction accuracy;
- calibration ranges instead of a single absolute score;
- category specialties after minimum samples;
- private “how your predictions changed” report;
- uncertainty rewarded when “Not sure” is honest, not penalized as failure;
- cooperative circle insight: “We understand travel choices better than impulse tech upgrades.”

### Mastery monetization

- advanced private calibration reports;
- historical filters and comparisons;
- professional presentation for creator communities;
- premium learning paths or challenge templates.

### Mastery guardrails

- no global leaderboard;
- no purchasable score boost;
- no financial prize tied to accuracy;
- no ranking based on very small samples;
- do not present social predictions as financial expertise.

## 10. Mechanics catalog

| Mechanic | Free core | Paid extension | Emotional payoff | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reveal | Every participant sees it | Premium presentation and archive tools | Curiosity, closure | Paywalling outcome |
| Prediction history | Basic recent record | Deep filters and calibration trends | Mastery | False precision |
| Circles | One functional circle | Multiple/custom circles and shared recap | Belonging | Payer control over data |
| Profile identity | Standard profile | Themes, frames, category cards | Vanity | Status hierarchy |
| Reflections | Always free | Narrative and long-range analysis | Satisfaction | Gating core value |
| Reactions | Standard set | Artist/creator packs | Expression | Childish tone |
| Challenges | Core pilot templates | Seasonal/creator packs | Belonging, progress | Compulsive activity |
| Appreciation | Free thanks | Optional paid gift | Reciprocity | Advice distortion |
| Creator room | Public sample | Paid membership/community | Influence, access | Hidden advertising |
| Annual recap | Basic summary | Premium designs/export | Pride, memory | Oversharing |

## 11. Ethical conversion moments

Appropriate places to present paid value:

- after a user has enough resolved history to understand the benefit;
- when viewing a basic recap, with a clear premium preview;
- while customizing a profile, circle, or Reveal;
- when an established circle asks for shared history or a seasonal recap;
- when a creator applies to host a paid community;
- when exporting or preserving a long-range personal archive.

Inappropriate places:

- while the user is emotionally uncertain about whether they can afford an item;
- immediately after a regretful reflection;
- before a participant can see a Reveal;
- inside a safety, report, block, delete, or cancellation flow;
- through a fake notification or invented scarcity;
- by implying that premium users make better decisions.

## 12. Emotion-safety review

Every monetized mechanic should answer:

1. What genuine user value exists without the emotional trigger?
2. Can a user decline without losing dignity, safety, or a promised outcome?
3. Does revenue increase when users buy more real-world products?
4. Does the mechanic reward completion and learning or merely activity volume?
5. Could it shame a non-paying user in front of friends?
6. Is paid status visually distinguishable from earned expertise?
7. Are commercial relationships visible before the user acts?
8. Can the user cancel, mute, hide, or remove the paid artifact easily?

If the answer reveals pressure, deception, or conflicted incentives, redesign or reject the mechanic.
